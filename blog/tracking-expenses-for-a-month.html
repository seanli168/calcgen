I Tracked Every Penny for 30 Days — What I Learned

Published: January 29, 2026 • By CalcGen

I thought I was pretty good with money. I wasn't living paycheck to paycheck. I had some savings. I didn't have credit card debt. But I also couldn't tell you exactly where my money went each month. So I decided to do an experiment: track every single dollar for 30 days.

Not budgeting. Not restricting. Just observing. Here's what happened.

The Method: Pen and a Notes App

I didn't use a fancy app. Every time I spent money — cash, card, Venmo, whatever — I wrote it down in a simple notes app. Date, amount, what it was for. That's it. If I bought a coffee, I wrote "Jan 5, $4.87, coffee." If I paid rent, "Jan 1, $1,250, rent."

At the end of the month, I categorized everything and tallied it up.

The Results: Where My $4,800 Went

My total spending for the month was about $4,800. Here's the breakdown:

  • Rent + utilities: $1,520 (32%)
  • Groceries: $520 (11%)
  • Restaurants + delivery: $610 (13%) — yes, more than groceries
  • Car payment + gas + insurance: $540 (11%)
  • Subscriptions + streaming: $85 (2%)
  • Misc shopping (Amazon, Target, etc): $380 (8%)
  • Entertainment + hobbies: $260 (5%)
  • Coffee shops: $95 (2%)
  • Everything else: $790 (16%)

The biggest shock: I spent more on restaurants and delivery ($610) than on actual groceries ($520). I was essentially buying groceries AND paying someone else to cook them. If I had cooked just half of those restaurant meals at home, I would have saved about $250-300 that month.

The Small Purchases That Added Up

Individually, nothing looked bad. A $4 coffee here. A $12 lunch there. A $25 impulse buy on Amazon. But when I saw them all added up at the end of the month, the total was sobering:

  • Coffee shops: 18 visits = $95. That's over $1,100 a year on coffee.
  • Convenience store snacks: $65. Chips, soda, candy bars — bought one at a time, never thinking about it.
  • App Store / in-app purchases: $34. I didn't even remember half of these.

None of these purchases were "bad." But they were unconscious. I was spending money without making a decision — I was just... swiping.

What Changed After the Experiment

I didn't cut everything. I still go out to eat, still buy coffee sometimes. But now I'm aware of it. I set a rough budget in my head: $200 for eating out, $100 for misc shopping. I don't track every penny anymore, but I check my spending once a week for five minutes. That alone keeps me from going overboard.

The biggest permanent change: I cook dinner at home Sunday through Thursday. Weekends, I eat out guilt-free. That one shift saves me about $250 a month — $3,000 a year — without feeling like a sacrifice.

What I'd recommend: Even if you only track for two weeks, do it. The awareness alone will change your behavior. You don't need a budget to spend less — you need to see where it's going.
