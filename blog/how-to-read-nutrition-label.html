How to Read a Nutrition Label (Without Confusion)

Published: June 2, 2026 • By CalcGen

For years, I'd look at nutrition labels and only check one thing: calories. If the calories were low enough, I'd buy it. I didn't understand macros, serving sizes, or why some foods with identical calories made me feel completely different. Learning to actually read labels took about 10 minutes and changed how I eat. Here's what I learned.

Start with Serving Size (The Trickiest Part)

This is where most people get fooled. A bag of chips might say "150 calories per serving" — but the bag contains 3 servings. Eat the whole thing, and you've consumed 450 calories. Always check the servings per container first. It's printed at the very top of the label for a reason.

Real example: A bottle of iced tea I used to buy said "80 calories per serving." Sounded healthy. Then I noticed it contained 2.5 servings per bottle. I was drinking 200 calories of sugar water every afternoon, thinking it was 80.

The Four Numbers I Actually Look At

I used to scan the entire label and retain nothing. Now I focus on four things, in order:

  • 1. Calories: Quick check — does this fit my daily target? I use our calorie calculator to know my numbers.
  • 2. Protein: I aim for at least 15-20g per meal. If a food has 3g of protein and 300 calories, it's probably not keeping me full.
  • 3. Added Sugar: This was the biggest revelation. The FDA now requires "Added Sugars" to be listed separately from total sugar. I try to keep added sugar under 25g per day. A single flavored yogurt can have 18g.
  • 4. Fiber: More fiber = more filling. I look for foods with at least 3g per serving.

That's it. Four numbers. If the calories fit, protein is decent, added sugar is low, and there's some fiber, I buy it. Everything else on the label is secondary for my goals.

The Ingredient List Hack

If the ingredient list is longer than about 10 items and half of them sound like chemistry lab supplies, I put it back. The simplest rule: food with fewer ingredients is usually better food. Peanut butter should say "peanuts, salt." Not "peanuts, sugar, hydrogenated vegetable oil, mono- and diglycerides, and five other things."
