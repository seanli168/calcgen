What Is BMI? Understanding Your Body Mass Index

Published: January 22, 2026 • By CalcGen

You've heard the term. Your doctor probably mentions it at every checkup. But what exactly is BMI, and does it actually matter?

BMI — Body Mass Index — is the most widely used screening tool for weight categories. It's simple, quick, and free. But it's also controversial, with critics pointing out that it doesn't tell the whole story about your health.

In this guide, you'll learn what BMI is, how it's calculated, what the categories mean, and — crucially — what BMI can't tell you.

What Is BMI?

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a number calculated from your height and weight. It's used as a screening tool to categorize people into weight groups: underweight, normal weight, overweight, or obese.

Key point: BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnostic tool. It flags potential weight-related health risks but doesn't diagnose anything on its own.

How Is BMI Calculated?

The formula is straightforward:

Metric: BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height² (m)

Imperial: BMI = [weight (lbs) ÷ height² (inches)] × 703

Example: A person weighing 70 kg (154 lbs) with a height of 1.75 m (5'9") has a BMI of 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 22.9, which falls in the healthy range.

BMI Categories: What the Numbers Mean

The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies BMI into these categories for adults 20 and older:

18.5 – 24.9Normal WeightLowest health risk
25.0 – 29.9OverweightIncreased risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease
30.0 – 34.9Obesity Class IHigh risk of cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea
35.0 – 39.9Obesity Class IIVery high risk
40.0 and aboveObesity Class IIIExtremely high risk (sometimes called "severe" or "morbid" obesity)

Why BMI Matters

Despite its limitations, BMI matters for two reasons:

  1. It's a population-level tool that works. Across large groups of people, higher BMI strongly correlates with higher rates of chronic disease, including diabetes, hypertension, and certain cancers. The relationship is so consistent that insurance companies, public health agencies, and medical researchers all use it.
  2. It starts a conversation. A BMI number alone isn't a diagnosis, but it opens the door for more detailed assessments — waist circumference measurement, blood tests, body fat percentage scans, and discussions about lifestyle factors.

The Limitations of BMI

BMI has well-known flaws that are important to understand:

1. It Doesn't Distinguish Muscle from Fat

This is the biggest criticism. Muscle weighs more than fat by volume, so a muscular athlete can have a BMI in the "overweight" or even "obese" range while having very low body fat. An NFL running back at 5'11" and 220 lbs has a BMI of 30.7 — "obese" — despite being an elite athlete with single-digit body fat.

2. It Doesn't Account for Age or Sex

Older adults naturally lose muscle mass and gain fat, even at the same BMI. Women carry more essential body fat than men. The same BMI number can mean different things for a 25-year-old man versus a 70-year-old woman.

3. It Doesn't Show Fat Distribution

Where you carry fat matters enormously. Visceral fat — the fat around your organs — is far more dangerous than subcutaneous fat stored under the skin. Two people with the same BMI can have completely different health risks depending on where their fat is stored. Waist circumference is often a better predictor of health risk than BMI alone.

4. It Was Developed from Limited Data

The BMI formula was created in the 1830s by a Belgian mathematician — not a physician — based on data from primarily white European men. It doesn't account for ethnic differences in body composition. For example, people of South Asian descent tend to have higher body fat at the same BMI compared to people of European descent, putting them at risk at lower BMI thresholds.

What BMI Should Be Used For — and What It Shouldn't

Use BMI for:

  • A quick, free screening tool
  • Tracking general population health trends
  • Starting a conversation with your healthcare provider
  • Motivating lifestyle changes when combined with other metrics

Don't use BMI for:

  • Diagnosing health conditions
  • Assessing athletes or very muscular individuals
  • Evaluating children (use age and sex-specific percentiles instead)
  • Making definitive judgments about someone's health or fitness

Better Alternatives and Complements to BMI

For a more complete picture of your health, combine BMI with one or more of these measurements:

  • Waist circumference: Measures abdominal fat. For most adults, health risks increase with waist measurements above 40 inches (102 cm) for men and 35 inches (88 cm) for women.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio: Compares waist to hip measurement. A ratio above 0.90 for men or 0.85 for women indicates higher risk.
  • Body fat percentage: Measured via DEXA scan, calipers, or bioelectrical impedance. Healthy ranges vary by age and sex.
  • Blood markers: Blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, and inflammatory markers give far more insight into your actual health than BMI alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is BMI accurate for everyone?

No. BMI is a screening tool with known limitations. It doesn't distinguish between muscle and fat, doesn't account for age, sex, or ethnicity, and doesn't show where fat is distributed. It's best used alongside other health measurements rather than as a standalone indicator of health.

Can I use this calculator for my child?

Our calculator is designed for adults 20 years and older. For children and teens (ages 2-19), BMI is interpreted differently using age and sex-specific growth charts called percentiles. A pediatrician can best assess a child's weight status.

What's a healthy BMI for my height?

A healthy BMI is 18.5 to 24.9 regardless of height, because the formula already accounts for height. That said, the weight range that corresponds to healthy BMI will be different for different heights. Our BMI Calculator automatically shows your healthy weight range.

If my BMI is high but I feel healthy, should I worry?

A high BMI with no symptoms doesn't necessarily mean you're unhealthy — especially if you're muscular. But it's worth discussing with your doctor. They may recommend additional measurements like waist circumference, blood tests, or body fat analysis to get a clearer picture.
