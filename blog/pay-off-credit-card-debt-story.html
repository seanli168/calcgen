How I Paid Off $8,000 in Credit Card Debt in 14 Months

Published: January 29, 2026 • By CalcGen

It started small. A car repair here, a weekend trip there. "I'll pay it off next month." Then next month came and there was another expense. Within two years, I had $8,000 spread across two credit cards, both at about 22% APR.

The minimum payments alone were $220 a month — and barely touching the principal. I did the math one night and realized that if I only paid minimums, I'd be paying for over 20 years and would end up paying nearly double the original amount in interest. That realization made me physically nauseous.

Here's how I got out — and what I wish I'd known sooner.

Step 1: I Called Both Card Companies

This was the scariest phone call I've ever made — and the most profitable. I called both card issuers and said basically: "I've been a customer for a few years. I'm struggling with the interest rate. Is there anything you can do?"

One card lowered my rate from 24.99% to 17.99% for six months. The other offered a temporary hardship program that reduced my minimum payment. Neither wanted me to default, so both were willing to work with me. Total savings from this phone call: about $800 in interest over the payoff period.

Lesson: Call your credit card company. The worst they can say is no. I was terrified to make that call, and it ended up saving me hundreds of dollars.

Step 2: I Used the Avalanche Method

I had two cards:

  • Card A: $3,200 at 24.99%
  • Card B: $4,800 at 22%

The avalanche method says: pay minimums on everything, then throw every extra dollar at the highest-rate card first. That meant Card A got attacked first. Mathematically, this saves the most on interest.

Honestly, the snowball method (paying the smallest balance first) might have been more motivating. Card A was smaller AND higher rate, so the methods aligned for me. But if Card B had been smaller, I probably would've done snowball instead — the psychological win of killing one card completely is real.

Step 3: I Found $570 a Month to Throw at Debt

This was the hard part. I made about $48,000 at the time — roughly $3,000 a month after taxes. Rent was $950. After essentials, I had maybe $400-500 left. I needed more.

  • Picked up weekend shifts at a friend's restaurant: ~$300/month. Two Saturday nights a month, bussing tables. Not glamorous, but it was cash.
  • Sold stuff I didn't use: Old phone, a guitar I never learned to play, extra furniture. ~$800 one-time.
  • Canceled everything non-essential: Gym membership I wasn't using ($40), streaming services I barely watched ($25), downgraded internet ($20).

Between the side hustle and the cuts, I freed up about $570 a month to put toward debt.

The Timeline

  • Months 1-2: Built a small $600 emergency fund first (so I wouldn't need the credit cards again). Paid minimums on both cards.
  • Months 3-8: Attacked Card A with everything extra. Paid $600-700/month. Cleared it in month 8.
  • Months 9-14: Rolled Card A's payment into Card B. Now paying $800-900/month. Cleared it in month 14.

Total interest paid: about $1,300. If I had stuck with minimum payments, I would've paid over $9,000 in interest alone. That thought still makes me angry.

What surprised me most: After month 3 or 4, it stopped feeling like a sacrifice. The momentum of seeing the balance drop was genuinely exciting. I started looking forward to making payments, which sounds insane but it's true.
