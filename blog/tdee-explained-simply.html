TDEE Confusion? How I Finally Figured Out My Calorie Needs

Published: January 29, 2026 • By CalcGen

When I first started trying to lose weight, I kept seeing this acronym everywhere: TDEE. Total Daily Energy Expenditure. Every fitness influencer talked about it like it was obvious. I had no idea what it meant or how to calculate mine.

I spent hours Googling, reading conflicting advice, trying different calculators that gave me wildly different numbers. One said I burned 2,800 calories a day. Another said 2,200. That's the difference between losing a pound a week and gaining weight.

Here's what I eventually figured out — explained the way I wish someone had explained it to me.

TDEE Is Just "How Many Calories You Burn in a Day"

That's it. No magic. No complicated science. Your TDEE is the total number of calories your body uses every 24 hours — including breathing, digesting food, walking to your car, and that workout you did. If you eat exactly your TDEE, you maintain your weight. Eat less, you lose. Eat more, you gain.

The simplest definition: TDEE = the calories you'd eat to stay exactly the same weight for a year.

The Two Parts: BMR + Everything Else

Think of your daily calorie burn in two buckets:

  • BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate): The calories you'd burn if you stayed in bed all day. This is what keeps your heart beating, lungs breathing, brain thinking. For most people, BMR is 60-70% of TDEE.
  • Everything else: Walking, typing, exercising, even fidgeting and digesting food. These add 30-40% on top of BMR.

Here's a real example. When I weighed 185 pounds at 5'10", my BMR was about 1,800 calories. I work a desk job but walk my dog and hit the gym 3-4 times a week. That activity adds roughly 600-700 calories. So my TDEE = 1,800 + 650 = about 2,450 calories/day.

Why Different Calculators Give Different Numbers

This drove me crazy until I understood it. Most calculators use one of three formulas — Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict, or Katch-McArdle. They all produce slightly different results because they were developed from different studies with different populations.

The Mifflin-St Jeor formula (what our calorie calculator uses) is considered the most accurate for most people. But even the best formula is an estimate. Your actual TDEE might be 100-200 calories higher or lower.

How I Found My Real TDEE (Not an Estimate)

I tracked my calories and weight every day for three weeks. I ate what I thought was maintenance, logged every gram, and weighed myself every morning. After three weeks, my weight was basically unchanged (fluctuated between 183-186). That confirmed my actual maintenance was right around 2,400-2,500.

Was it tedious? Yes. But I only had to do it once. Now I know my real number — not a calculator's guess.

The takeaway: Online calculators give you a starting point. Your actual TDEE might differ slightly. Track for 2-3 weeks to confirm, then adjust from there.
