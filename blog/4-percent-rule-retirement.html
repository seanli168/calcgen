The 4% Rule Explained: How Much Do You Really Need to Retire?

Published: January 22, 2026 • By CalcGen

Ask ten people how much money they need to retire, and you'll get ten different answers. But there's one number that comes up more than any other: 4%.

The 4% rule is the most famous guideline in retirement planning. It's the backbone of the FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) movement. It's quoted by financial advisors, debated on Reddit, and used by millions of people to set their retirement savings target.

But what exactly is it? Where did it come from? And — most importantly — can you actually rely on it?

What Is the 4% Rule?

The 4% rule says: In your first year of retirement, withdraw 4% of your total savings. Every year after, adjust that dollar amount for inflation.

If you follow this rule, your money should last at least 30 years — and in most historical scenarios, much longer.

Where Did the 4% Rule Come From?

The rule originated from the Trinity Study, published in 1998 by three finance professors at Trinity University. The study asked a simple question: if you retired with a portfolio of 50% stocks and 50% bonds, withdrew a fixed percentage each year (adjusted for inflation), what were your odds of running out of money over 30 years?

They tested withdrawal rates from 3% to 12% across every 30-year period from 1926 to 1995 — including the Great Depression, World War II, the 1970s stagflation, and the 1987 crash.

The result: a 4% withdrawal rate had a 95%+ success rate over 30-year periods. In most periods, you actually ended up with more money than you started with.

How the 4% Rule Works in Practice

Let's say you retire with $1,500,000:

  • Year 1: Withdraw $60,000 (4% of $1.5M)
  • Year 2: Withdraw $61,800 (assuming 3% inflation)
  • Year 3: Withdraw $63,654
  • And so on...

You withdraw the same inflation-adjusted amount every year, regardless of what the market does. In good years, your portfolio grows and creates a buffer. In bad years, you draw down principal — but the buffer from good years typically covers the dips.

Is the 4% Rule Still Safe Today?

This is where the debate gets heated. Critics raise three main concerns:

1. Lower Expected Returns

When the Trinity Study was published, bond yields were 5-6%. Today, long-term bond yields are lower, and some analysts expect lower equity returns over the coming decades. If future returns are lower than historical averages, 4% might be too aggressive.

2. Longer Retirements

The Trinity Study assumed a 30-year retirement. But if you retire at 50 and live to 90+, you need your money to last 40+ years. At 4%, the 40-year success rate drops to about 87% — not terrible, but not the near-certainty of 30 years.

3. Sequence of Returns Risk

The biggest danger to a 4% withdrawal strategy isn't the average return — it's bad returns in the first few years of retirement. If the market drops 30% right after you retire and you keep withdrawing, your portfolio might never recover.

The counterargument: The 4% rule already survived the worst periods in modern history — including retiring in 1929 (right before the Great Depression) and 1966 (right before 15 years of stagflation). In both cases, 4% worked. That's a pretty strong track record.

Modern Adjustments to the 4% Rule

Given the concerns, many experts now recommend modifications:

  • Use 3.5% for early retirement (40+ years). More conservative, higher success rate.
  • Use 3.0% for ultra-early retirement (50+ years). Near-guaranteed success.
  • Use a flexible withdrawal strategy. Spend less in down years, more in up years.
  • Use a "guardrails" approach. Cut spending when your portfolio drops below a threshold, increase when it exceeds.

How to Calculate Your Personal Retirement Number

  1. Estimate your annual retirement expenses. Track your current spending. Subtract costs that will disappear (commuting, mortgage if paid off). Add costs that will increase (travel, healthcare).
  2. Subtract guaranteed income. Social Security, pensions, annuities reduce how much your portfolio needs to cover.
  3. Multiply by 25 (for 4%) or 28.5 (for 3.5%). This is your target nest egg.

Example: You need $60,000/year in retirement. Social Security provides $20,000/year. Your portfolio needs to generate $40,000/year. At 4%: $40,000 × 25 = $1,000,000. At a more conservative 3.5%: $40,000 × 28.5 = $1,140,000.

The 4% Rule Is a Guideline, Not a Law

The 4% rule's greatest value isn't as a precise withdrawal strategy — it's as a planning tool. It gives you a target to aim for. It turns "I want to retire comfortably" into "I need approximately $1.5 million saved."

In practice, almost no one withdraws exactly 4% plus inflation every year. Real retirees adjust their spending — less in market downturns, more when the portfolio is doing well. The 4% rule is the starting point, not the final answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the 4% rule account for inflation?

Yes. The rule says you increase your dollar withdrawals by inflation each year. If you withdrew $60,000 in year one and inflation was 3%, you withdraw $61,800 in year two. This maintains your purchasing power.

Does the 4% rule include taxes?

No. The 4% rule deals with gross withdrawals. You need to account for taxes separately. Money in Roth accounts comes out tax-free, while traditional 401(k)/IRA withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Factor taxes into your expense estimate.

What if I retire early — does the 4% rule still work?

For retirements longer than 30 years, 4% has a lower success rate. The FIRE community often uses 3.5% or 3.25% for early retirement. The longer your retirement, the more conservative your withdrawal rate should be.

Can I use the 4% rule if I'm not retiring yet?

Absolutely. The 4% rule is best used as a savings target. Figure out your annual retirement spending, multiply by 25, and that's your goal. Then use our Retirement Calculator to see if your current savings rate gets you there.
