How Much House Can You Afford? A Complete Guide

Published: January 22, 2026 • By CalcGen

Your dream home shows up on Zillow. It's perfect — the right neighborhood, the right size, the right vibe. But before you schedule a showing, there's one question you need to answer: Can you actually afford it?

Too many homebuyers rely on what a lender approves them for — which is often far more than what's financially comfortable. This guide walks you through exactly how to calculate what you can afford, using the same methods financial advisors and mortgage underwriters use.

The 28/36 Rule: Your Starting Point

The 28/36 rule is the gold standard for home affordability. It states:

  • 28% Rule: Your total monthly housing costs (mortgage + property tax + insurance + HOA) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income.
  • 36% Rule: Your total monthly debt payments (housing + car loans + student loans + credit cards) should not exceed 36% of your gross monthly income.

Quick example: If your household earns $8,000/month before taxes, your housing budget should stay under $2,240/month (28%), and all debt payments combined under $2,880/month (36%).

Calculate Your Maximum Home Price

Let's walk through a real calculation. Assume:

  • Monthly gross income: $8,000
  • Down payment: 20% ($70,000 saved)
  • Interest rate: 6.5%
  • Loan term: 30 years
  • Property tax: 1.1% of home value per year
  • Home insurance: $1,200/year

Step 1: Calculate Your Maximum Monthly Payment

28% of $8,000 = $2,240/month maximum for housing.

Step 2: Subtract Taxes and Insurance

Taxes and insurance eat into your housing budget before the mortgage itself. On a $350,000 home:

  • Annual property tax: $350,000 × 1.1% = $3,850 → $321/month
  • Home insurance: $1,200/year → $100/month
  • Remaining for mortgage (P&I): $2,240 - $321 - $100 = $1,819/month

Step 3: Work Backwards to the Loan Amount

A $1,819 monthly payment at 6.5% for 30 years corresponds to a loan of approximately $288,000.

Step 4: Add Your Down Payment

Maximum home price = Loan amount + Down payment = $288,000 + $70,000 = $358,000.

Result: With $8,000 monthly income and a 20% down payment, you can comfortably afford a home up to approximately $358,000. Use our Mortgage Calculator to run your own numbers.

Beyond the 28/36 Rule: What Lenders Actually Look At

Mortgage lenders use DTI (Debt-to-Income) ratio, which is very similar to the 28/36 rule but with their own thresholds:

  • Front-end DTI (housing only): Most lenders want this under 28-31%.
  • Back-end DTI (all debts): Most conventional loans cap this at 36-43%. FHA loans may go up to 50% in some cases.

Lenders also look at your credit score, employment history, and cash reserves. A higher credit score qualifies you for better rates, which directly increases your purchasing power.

The Down Payment Factor

Your down payment dramatically affects what you can afford:

  • 20% down: No PMI required, best rates, lower monthly payments.
  • 10% down: PMI required (~0.5-1% of loan annually), slightly higher rate.
  • 5% down: Higher PMI, possible rate increase, less equity cushion.
  • 3% down (FHA): Highest insurance costs (MIP), but lowest barrier to entry.

PMI alert: On a $300,000 loan with 5% down, PMI could cost $150-250/month. That's money that doesn't build equity or pay down your loan. Whenever possible, aim for 20% down to avoid PMI entirely.

Hidden Costs First-Time Buyers Forget

The mortgage payment is just the start. Budget for these often-overlooked expenses:

  • Closing costs: 2-5% of the home price, due at signing.
  • Maintenance: Budget 1-2% of home value annually for repairs.
  • Utilities: Larger than an apartment — potentially $200-500/month more.
  • HOA fees: Can range from $50/month to $500+ depending on the community.
  • Furnishings and upgrades: The first year of homeownership often brings surprise expenses.

A good rule: if your mortgage payment is $2,000, budget an additional $400-600/month for maintenance, utilities, and unexpected costs.

5 Steps to Determine Your Real Budget

  1. Calculate 28% of your gross monthly income. This is your absolute ceiling for housing costs.
  2. Subtract your non-housing debts. If your total debts exceed 36% of income, you need to pay down debt or adjust your housing target down.
  3. Get pre-approved, not just pre-qualified. Pre-approval involves actual income verification and gives you a realistic number.
  4. Test-drive the payment. For 2-3 months, set aside the difference between your current rent and your target mortgage payment. If it feels tight, adjust your budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's more important — the home price or the monthly payment?

The monthly payment. A $400,000 home with 20% down might cost $2,500/month, while a $350,000 home with 5% down could cost the same due to PMI and a larger loan. Always focus on the monthly cash flow impact.

Should I buy at the top of my budget?

Generally no. Lenders often approve you for more than what's comfortable. Buying at 80-90% of your maximum leaves room for life changes, repairs, and savings. A mortgage that feels manageable now shouldn't become a burden if interest rates rise or income changes.

How much should I save for a down payment?

20% is ideal — it eliminates PMI and gives you instant equity. But in high-cost areas, 10% or even 5% is common. The key is having enough cash left after closing for an emergency fund of 3-6 months' expenses.

Does my credit score really matter that much?

Yes. On a $300,000 30-year loan, a 760 score might qualify for 6.5% while a 620 score might see 7.5%+. That 1% difference means roughly $200 more per month and $72,000 more in total interest. Improving your credit before buying is one of the highest-return moves you can make.
