How to Negotiate a Raise: The Script I Actually Used

Published: May 30, 2026 • By CalcGen

Last fall, I asked for a raise for the first time in my life. I was terrified. I rehearsed in front of my mirror. I almost backed out three times. But I did it — and walked out with a 14% increase. Here's exactly how I prepared, what I said, and what I learned.

The Preparation (2 Weeks Before)

I didn't just walk in and ask for more money. I spent two weeks gathering ammunition:

  • Market research: I looked up salary data for my role and location on Glassdoor, LinkedIn Salary, and Levels.fyi. I found that the median for my position was about $8,000 more than I was making.
  • My accomplishments log: I went through my calendar and emails from the past year and wrote down every project I'd led, every metric I'd improved, and every piece of positive feedback I'd received. Specific numbers. Not "I did a good job" — "I led the website redesign that increased conversions by 18%."
  • Timing: I scheduled the meeting for a Wednesday morning (studies show midweek mornings have higher success rates for negotiations) and about six weeks before annual review season, so my manager could factor it into budget planning.

Key prep step: I practiced saying my number out loud at least 20 times. The first 15 times, it felt ridiculous. By #20, it was just a sentence. Desensitizing yourself to the ask is half the battle.

The Script I Used

Here's almost exactly what I said. I kept it to about 90 seconds — long enough to be substantive, short enough not to ramble.

"Thanks for making time. I wanted to talk about my compensation. Over the past year, I've led the website redesign that increased conversions by 18%, mentored two junior team members, and consistently received positive feedback on client projects. Based on my contributions and market research for this role, I'd like to discuss an adjustment to $82,000. Is that something we can work toward?"

Notice what I didn't say: I didn't mention inflation or rising cost of living. I didn't compare myself to coworkers. I didn't threaten to leave. I focused entirely on the value I'd created and the market rate for that value.

What Happened Next

My manager said she'd need to discuss with HR. I said that was completely fine and asked when I could follow up. She suggested a week. Exactly one week later, she came back with an offer: they couldn't do $82,000 immediately, but they could do $79,500 — a 14% increase — with a commitment to revisit at my annual review in three months.

I accepted. It wasn't my full ask, but $6,500 more than I was making. For a 15-minute conversation and two weeks of prep, that's a phenomenal hourly rate.

What I'd Do Differently

I wish I'd asked sooner. I was underpaid for at least 18 months because I was too scared to have the conversation. The anxiety I built up in my head was 100 times worse than the actual meeting. My manager was professional, not offended. She'd done this before. I was the only one treating it like a confrontation.
