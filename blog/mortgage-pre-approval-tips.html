My Mortgage Pre-Approval Journey: What Actually Matters

Published: January 29, 2026 • By CalcGen

My wife and I decided to buy our first home last year. Everyone said "get pre-approved first, then shop." So I filled out an online application in 10 minutes, got a letter that said we were approved for $450,000, and thought we were done.

I was wrong. Getting "pre-approved" and actually understanding what you can afford are two completely different things. Here's what I learned — including a mistake that almost cost us the house.

Pre-Qualified vs Pre-Approved: Not the Same Thing

The first thing I learned: there are two levels. Pre-qualification is what I did first — enter your income and assets online, get an instant estimate. It's based on what you tell them. They don't verify anything. It's about as meaningful as a handshake.

Pre-approval is the real thing. They pull your credit, verify your income with pay stubs and tax returns, check your bank statements. This takes 1-2 weeks and the letter actually means something to sellers.

What I wish I knew: Sellers take pre-approval letters seriously. A pre-qualification letter? Not so much. If you're serious about buying, get the real pre-approval.

The Number They Give You Is Usually Too High

The bank approved us for $450,000. Our combined income was about $110,000 at the time. On paper, the math worked — our debt-to-income ratio was under the limit.

But here's what the bank doesn't consider: we wanted to keep contributing to our retirement accounts. We wanted to be able to go on a vacation once a year. We didn't want to be "house poor" — where the mortgage takes up so much of your income that everything else feels tight.

We ended up buying a house for $320,000. About $130,000 less than we were "approved" for. Our mortgage payment (including taxes and insurance) is about $2,100 a month — roughly 23% of our gross income. That's under the 28% rule, and it feels comfortable. We can still save, still travel, still breathe.

The biggest mistake people make: They look at their pre-approval number and think "that's my budget." It's not. That's the bank's maximum. Your real budget is probably 20-30% less if you want to keep living your life.

The Credit Score Surprise

Three months before applying, I checked my credit score on a free app and it showed 720. When the lender pulled it, it came back as 687. Different scoring model. That 33-point difference meant a slightly higher interest rate — about 0.25% higher.

On a $320,000 loan, that 0.25% difference adds about $50 a month and roughly $18,000 over 30 years. Not life-changing, but I wish I had checked my actual FICO score (not the free app version) months before applying. I could have paid down a credit card balance to bump my score before the lender pulled it.

What Actually Happens During Pre-Approval

Here's the process as I experienced it:

  1. Application: Basic info about income, employment, assets, debts. Took about 30 minutes.
  2. Document collection: They asked for 2 years of tax returns, 2 months of pay stubs, 2 months of bank statements, and a letter explaining a large deposit (my tax refund). This was the most annoying part.
  3. Credit pull: They check all three bureaus and use the middle score.
  4. Underwriting: An actual human reviews everything. Took about a week.
  5. Letter issued: States the loan amount, type, and expiration date (ours was good for 90 days).

The Mistake I Almost Made

While we were house shopping — after pre-approval but before closing — I almost bought a new car. My old one was acting up and a dealership had a good financing deal. I mentioned it to our loan officer and she literally said "Do not do that. Do not open any new credit. Do not make any large purchases. Do not even think about it until after closing."

Apparently lenders re-check your credit right before closing. If I had opened a car loan, my debt-to-income ratio would have changed, and we could have lost the mortgage approval entirely. I had no idea.

Between pre-approval and closing: Don't open new credit cards, don't finance a car, don't make large purchases that drain your cash reserves. Just... freeze your finances until you get the keys.
