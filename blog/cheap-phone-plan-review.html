I Switched to a Cheap Phone Plan — Was the Service Actually Worse?

Published: June 1, 2026 • By CalcGen

I paid $85 a month to Verizon for almost a decade. I assumed the high price meant better service — and the one time I tried a cheaper carrier in 2018, the coverage was terrible and I switched back within two months. But a lot has changed since then. The same networks that power the expensive plans now sell access to smaller carriers at a fraction of the cost.

So I tried again. I switched to a $25/month plan that uses the same Verizon towers I'd been paying $85 for. Here's what happened.

What I Actually Got for $25/Month

I went with Visible, which is actually owned by Verizon and runs on their network. $25/month for unlimited talk, text, and data. No contract. I ordered the SIM card online, it arrived in two days, and activation took about ten minutes. I kept my same phone number.

The biggest difference on paper: data prioritization. My old Verizon plan had "premium data" that didn't slow down during congestion. The $25 plan has "deprioritized data" — meaning if the network is busy (like at a stadium or during rush hour), my speeds might slow down.

What Real-World Usage Was Like

Honestly? I couldn't tell the difference 95% of the time. I stream music on my commute, scroll social media, use GPS, make calls — all of it worked exactly the same as before. I ran a few speed tests out of curiosity. At 3pm on a Tuesday, my download speed was about 40 Mbps — fast enough to stream HD video. My old Verizon plan got about 80 Mbps. But on a phone screen, you can't tell the difference between 40 and 80 Mbps. They both load Instagram instantly.

The one time I noticed the deprioritization was at a packed farmers market on a Saturday morning. My data slowed to a crawl — maybe 2-3 Mbps — while my friend on premium Verizon was still getting decent speeds. It was annoying but temporary. For the other 167 hours of the week, service was indistinguishable.

Annual savings: $85 − $25 = $60/month × 12 = $720 per year. Over five years, that's $3,600 — and that's assuming the price never goes up. I used our compound interest calculator to see what investing that $60/month would do. At 7% over 20 years: over $31,000. For making one phone call to switch carriers.

What to Watch Out For

  • Check which network the cheap carrier uses. Not all MVNOs use Verizon. Some use T-Mobile or AT&T. Look up coverage maps for your area before switching.
  • Phone compatibility. Most newer phones work, but if you have an older or locked device, check compatibility on the carrier's website before ordering.
  • Customer support is minimal. At $25/month, you're not getting 24/7 phone support. Visible is chat-only. I had one issue (SIM wouldn't activate) and it was resolved through chat in about 20 minutes. Not instant, but adequate.
  • Family plans vs single lines. If you have four or five lines on a family plan, the per-line cost on a major carrier might already be close to $25-30, in which case switching might not save much. Do the math first.
