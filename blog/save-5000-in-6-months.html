I Saved $5,000 in 6 Months — Here's Exactly How I Did It

Published: January 29, 2026 • By CalcGen

In January this year, I checked my savings account and felt sick. After years of working, I had less than $1,000 set aside. A car repair or a medical bill would've wiped me out completely.

So I made a decision: I was going to save $5,000 by July. Not "try to save." Actually save it. Here's exactly what happened — the good, the embarrassing, and what actually worked.

The Starting Point (It Wasn't Pretty)

I make about $55,000 a year, which comes out to roughly $3,400 a month after taxes and health insurance. My rent was $1,250. Car payment $320. Student loans $280. After groceries, gas, and "miscellaneous" spending, I was saving maybe $100-200 a month on a good month.

$5,000 in six months meant I needed to save $834 per month. That was more than my car payment. It felt impossible on paper.

Month 1: The Reality Check

I printed out three months of bank statements and highlighted every single transaction. Here's what I found:

  • Food delivery: $340/month on average. I was ordering DoorDash 3-4 times a week and hadn't even realized it.
  • Subscriptions I forgot about: $47/month. A streaming service I hadn't used in 8 months, a meditation app I used twice, and a "premium" version of something I couldn't even remember signing up for.
  • Random Amazon purchases: $120-180/month on stuff I barely remember ordering.

That was $500+ a month right there — just leaking out of my account without me noticing.

First lesson: You don't need more income to start saving. You need to plug the leaks first. I found $500/month just by looking at where money was already going.

Month 2-3: The Actual Cuts

I made three specific changes:

  • Deleted DoorDash. Not "used it less." Deleted the app entirely. I started meal prepping on Sundays — nothing fancy, just chicken, rice, and vegetables in containers. Grocery bill went up by about $80, but food delivery went from $340 to $0. Net savings: $260/month.
  • Called my internet provider. I literally said "I'm thinking of switching, can you check if I qualify for any promotions?" They reduced my bill from $75 to $50. Five minute phone call, $25/month saved.
  • Switched to a cheaper phone plan. From $70 to $40 by going with an MVNO instead of the big carrier. Same network, $30 less.

Those three changes freed up $315/month.

Month 4: The Side Hustle Experiment

Even with the cuts, I was still about $400 short of my monthly goal. So I started doing DoorDash — the irony wasn't lost on me. Two evenings a week and one weekend morning. Averaged about $18-20/hour after gas. In a typical week, that was an extra $150-180.

Was it fun? No. But knowing it was temporary made it doable. I kept telling myself: "This isn't forever. This is until July."

Month 5: The Surprise Expense

Of course, life happened. My car needed new brake pads and rotors — $480. Old me would've put it on a credit card. This time, I had enough in my savings to pay cash. The relief of not going into debt for a car repair was honestly worth more than the $5,000 goal itself.

I ended that month a little behind target, but I didn't quit. I picked up an extra DoorDash shift to make up the difference.

Month 6: Crossing the Finish Line

By the last week of June, my savings account balance read $5,170. I'd actually exceeded my goal by a little bit. Here's the breakdown of where the money came from:

  • Cutting food delivery: ~$1,560
  • Lowering bills (internet + phone): ~$330
  • Canceled subscriptions: ~$282
  • Side hustle (DoorDash): ~$2,400
  • Reduced random spending: ~$600

Biggest surprise: After the first month, I stopped missing most of the things I cut. Meal prepping became routine. I didn't miss the streaming service I canceled. The "sacrifices" stopped feeling like sacrifices.

What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

  1. Print your bank statements first. You can't fix what you don't see. I was genuinely shocked at how much I spent on delivery.
  2. Start with the easy wins. Canceled subscriptions and bill negotiations take maybe an hour total and save hundreds.
  3. Make it automatic. I set up an auto-transfer of $300 every paycheck to a separate savings account. If I didn't see it, I couldn't spend it.
  4. Give yourself a timeline. "I'll save more" is vague. "I'll save $5,000 by July 1st" is specific. The deadline kept me going.
