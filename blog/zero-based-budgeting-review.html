Zero-Based Budgeting: I Tried It for 3 Months — Honest Review

Published: May 30, 2026 • By CalcGen

Zero-based budgeting (ZBB) has a cult following in personal finance communities. The idea is simple: give every dollar a job. Your income minus your expenses (including savings and investments) should equal exactly zero. Not because you spent everything — because every dollar is assigned somewhere, even if that "somewhere" is a savings account.

I tried it for three months. Here's what worked, what drove me crazy, and whether I stuck with it.

How Zero-Based Budgeting Works

You start with your monthly take-home pay. Then you assign every single dollar to a category until there's nothing left:

  • Income: $4,000
  • Rent: $1,200
  • Groceries: $400
  • Utilities: $180
  • Car + gas: $350
  • Eating out: $200
  • Shopping: $100
  • Emergency fund: $500
  • Roth IRA: $583
  • Entertainment: $150
  • Misc: $337 (the "buffer")

Result: $4,000 − $4,000 = $0. Every dollar has a destination before the month begins.

What I Loved About ZBB

Intentionality. This is the core benefit. With ZBB, you can't spend mindlessly. That $40 Amazon purchase? It has to come from somewhere. You either planned for it in "Shopping" or you have to steal from "Eating Out" to cover it. The trade-offs become visible.

Before ZBB, I had a rough budget in my head — but there was always "miscellaneous money" that mysteriously disappeared by month-end. ZBB eliminated that gray area. I found about $300/month that I'd been losing to nothing — not restaurants, not shopping, just... nothing. ATM withdrawals, small purchases, things I couldn't even remember.

What I Hated About ZBB

The maintenance is tedious. Every transaction has to be categorized. If you go $40 over on groceries, you have to decide which category to pull $40 from. It's a constant game of adjustment. By month two, I was spending 15-20 minutes every few days just updating my budget spreadsheet.

ZBB is not for people with irregular income. If your paycheck varies month to month — freelancer, commission-based, seasonal worker — zero-based budgeting is significantly harder. You're constantly adjusting your plan based on income you can't predict. The 50/30/20 rule or a simple expense-tracking approach is more practical for variable incomes.

What I Settled On After 3 Months

I don't do strict ZBB anymore, but I kept the best part: assigning savings and investments first. That's the real power of ZBB — it forces you to prioritize. Before anything else, $500 goes to emergency fund, $583 goes to Roth IRA. What's left is for living.

Now I use a hybrid: ZBB for savings and fixed expenses, looser tracking for variable spending. I check our percentage calculator monthly to make sure my broad allocations (needs/wants/savings) are in line with the 50/30/20 rule. If they are, I don't stress about the individual categories.
